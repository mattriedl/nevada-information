Nevada Hot Springs: The Best Resorts, Free Springs and Soaks Near Reno and Las Vegas

Developed resorts near Reno and Carson City, free primitive springs in the desert, and the hard canyon route to the closest hot spring to Las Vegas.

Nevada hot springs come in two kinds: a handful of developed resorts near Reno and Carson City where you pay for a two-hour soak in clean, managed pools, and dozens of free, primitive springs on public land in the desert, from cattle-trough tubs near Austin to a slot-canyon spring outside Las Vegas. Nevada has more hot springs than any other state, according to Travel Nevada, and late fall through spring is the best season to visit most of them. This guide covers the best ones to soak in, what they cost, how to reach them and how to stay safe.

Quick answer

  • Easiest soak: Carson Hot Springs in Carson City, open 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. daily, $18 for two hours in the outdoor pools.
  • Near Reno: Steamboat Hot Springs Healing Center on the south edge of Reno (mineral baths from $25, book ahead) and David Walley's in Genoa.
  • Near Las Vegas: Gold Strike Hot Springs, a very strenuous 5.3-mile round-trip canyon route near Boulder City, closed every year from May 15 to Sept. 30.
  • Best free springs: Spencer Hot Springs near Austin, Fish Lake Valley Hot Well near Dyer and Soldier Meadows north of Gerlach.
  • Safety: wild springs are untested and can carry Naegleria and bacteria. Keep your head above water and never drink it.

How many hot springs are in Nevada?

Nevada has more hot springs than any other state, though only a few dozen are realistic places to soak. Travel Nevada, the state tourism office, describes Nevada as "overflowing with hot springs," with thermal springs scattered across nearly every corner of the Great Basin. The heat comes from the same faulting that built Nevada's mountain ranges: groundwater circulates deep along fault lines, heats up and rises back to the surface.

Many of those springs are too hot, too small, on private ranch land or protected for endangered fish. The ones worth planning a trip around fall into three groups:

  • Resorts and spas in the Reno, Carson City and Carson Valley area, with staffed pools, locker rooms and posted hours.
  • Primitive public-land springs managed by the Bureau of Land Management (BLM), a county or the National Park Service, usually free, with no services.
  • Backcountry springs that take a hard hike, a boat or a long dirt-road drive.

What are the best hot springs near Reno and Carson City?

The best hot springs near Reno are three developed resorts within about an hour of downtown: Steamboat in south Reno, Carson Hot Springs in Carson City and David Walley's in Genoa. All three are open year-round and work well on a winter day when the Sierra is snowy. There are no good free soaking springs close to Reno, so these are the realistic choices for a quick trip.

Carson Hot Springs, Carson City

The most popular soak in northern Nevada sits on the northeast side of Carson City, about 30 miles south of Reno. Water flows out of the ground at 121 degrees and is cooled into four outdoor pools that run 98 to 104 degrees year-round, plus nine private indoor pool rooms at 100 to 104 degrees, according to Carson Hot Springs. The pools are a flow-through system, drained and refilled daily, with no chemicals or city water added.

Admission is for two hours. The outdoor pools and patio cost $18 for adults (ages 12 to 54) and $16 for seniors 55 and over and kids 2 to 11; private indoor pools, which include the outdoor pools and sauna, cost $32 for adults. Reservations are not taken, even for private rooms, so arrive early on winter weekends. Travel Nevada notes that the outdoor area is capped at 50 people. Alcohol and coolers are not allowed, towels are rented rather than included, and there is a restaurant and brewery on the property.

Steamboat Hot Springs Healing Center and Spa, Reno

Steamboat sits on a geothermal area on the south edge of Reno along South Virginia Street. It is more spa than pool: you book a private mineral bath or an outdoor mineral bath paired with a cold shower. Prices start at $25 for the outdoor bath and $30 for a private bath, with 3- and 6-visit packs, according to Steamboat Hot Springs.

Book at least 48 hours ahead; a credit card holds the time, and mineral baths can be canceled up to 2 hours before without a fee. Children under 12 are not allowed, and guests 12 to 17 need a parent or guardian. Swimsuits are required in shared soaks.

David Walley's Hot Springs Resort, Genoa

About a mile south of Genoa, Nevada's oldest town, at the foot of the Carson Range, David Walley's is a historic spring turned timeshare resort. David and Harriet Walley opened a spa here in 1862 with 11 beds, a ballroom and gardens; the original complex burned in 1935, according to the Nevada State Historic Preservation Office marker text.

Today the resort, run by Holiday Inn Club Vacations, has five mineral hot spring tubs at 98 to 104 degrees, one of them set aside for families with kids, plus a heated outdoor pool. Hours are 7 a.m. to 10 p.m. daily (Holiday Inn Club Vacations). Non-guests can buy hot springs day use, and reservations are not needed, according to Visit Lake Tahoe; entry includes the steam rooms, saunas and locker rooms. The resort does not post day-use prices online, so call ahead. It is a cashless property.

Are there hot springs near Las Vegas?

Yes, but they are not easy: the hot springs near Las Vegas sit in the Black Canyon of the Colorado River below Hoover Dam, about an hour from the Strip, and reaching them on foot means a hard canyon hike. All of them are in Lake Mead National Recreation Area. There are no developed hot spring resorts in the Las Vegas area.

Gold Strike Hot Springs (Goldstrike Canyon)

Gold Strike is the closest natural hot spring to Las Vegas, on the Nevada side of the river. The trailhead is a free parking area off Goldstrike Road near Boulder City, just before the Mike O'Callaghan-Pat Tillman Memorial Bridge. The National Park Service rates it "very strenuous," a Class 2 canyoneering route of 5.3 miles round trip with about 1,200 feet of elevation change and seven dry falls, taking 4 to 8 hours.

Take that rating seriously. The route is not maintained, and the ropes you will find on the dry falls are left by other hikers; the park says they should not be relied on and asks you to check whether you could climb back up without one before going down. Every year hikers underestimate it, leading to injuries and deaths. The springs are not tested or designated swim areas, and the park warns they can contain Naegleria and high levels of fecal bacteria.

The canyon closes every year from May 15 to Sept. 30 because of heat, and it can also close when temperatures at Willow Beach are forecast to reach 95 degrees or flash floods are possible. The springs stay reachable by boat or kayak from the river. Gold Strike is in a fee area: a 7-day vehicle pass for Lake Mead costs $25 (NPS fees). Bring at least 2 liters of water per person, and leave dogs at home.

Arizona Hot Spring

Across the river in Arizona, the Arizona Hot Spring is reached from the White Rock Canyon trailhead off US 93 on the Arizona side of the bridge. It falls under the same summer closure: in 2024, for example, the park closed White Rock Canyon, the Arizona Hot Springs Trail and Goldstrike Canyon from May 15 to Sept. 30 (NPS). Check current conditions with the park at 702-293-8990 before any trip into Black Canyon.

What are the best free hot springs in Nevada?

The best free hot springs in Nevada are Spencer Hot Springs in central Nevada, Fish Lake Valley Hot Well near the California line and Soldier Meadows in the Black Rock country. All three are primitive, open year-round and a long drive from any city, so they suit a road trip or a camping weekend rather than a quick soak.

Spencer Hot Springs, near Austin

Spencer is the classic Nevada roadside soak: a cluster of hot runoff pools in the high desert southeast of Austin in Lander County. The water leaves the source at around 130 degrees and cools to the low 100s in the soaking tubs. There are three, sometimes four, places to soak: two metal cattle troughs, one natural soft-bottomed pool and a cooler cowboy tub, according to Travel Nevada.

It is free and open 24 hours, on BLM land. There are no toilets, drinking water or other services, the BLM says, so bring what you need and pack everything out. Primitive camping is first-come, first-served; keep your camp at least 100 yards from the water. Most visitors come from US 50 near Austin via State Route 376 and a few miles of gravel road; get current directions from Austin Tourism (775-964-2200) and a paper map, since cell coverage is patchy.

Fish Lake Valley Hot Well, near Dyer

In Esmeralda County, Nevada's least populated county, the Fish Lake Valley Hot Well is a concrete tub fed by a strong flow that stays around 105 degrees, with outflow running into natural ponds. It is about 20 minutes from Dyer on a county-maintained dirt road, or a little over an hour from Tonopah on dirt and gravel mining roads (Travel Nevada). Esmeralda County manages the site; it is free, with a large parking area, primitive campsites and a vault toilet. Camp at least 100 feet from the spring, and leave the fish alone: the ponds hold the critically endangered Fish Lake Valley tui chub.

Soldier Meadows, Black Rock Desert

Soldier Meadows is a natural hot creek and a string of pools about 60 miles north of Gerlach, roughly five hours from Reno, in the Black Rock Desert-High Rock Canyon Emigrant Trails National Conservation Area. Water runs from the mid-90s to the low 100s, with no troughs or concrete. The last stretch is about 2.5 hours on a sometimes-rough road, and Travel Nevada recommends a high-clearance 4WD vehicle (Travel Nevada). The BLM keeps free, semi-primitive campsites and a first-come, first-served cabin with a wood stove nearby. Don't use sunscreen or soap in the water: the pools are home to the threatened desert dace. There is no cell service, so call Friends of Black Rock (775-557-2900) in Gerlach for road and playa conditions before you go.

Where are Nevada's hot springs? A regional map in words

Most of Nevada's soakable hot springs cluster in the west and center of the state, along the faults of the Great Basin. If you are looking at a Nevada hot springs map, this is the quick way to read it:

  • Reno-Tahoe and western Nevada: the developed resorts, Steamboat, Carson and Walley's, all within an hour of each other.
  • Black Rock country (Washoe and Humboldt counties): Soldier Meadows and other wild springs north of Gerlach, reached on long dirt roads.
  • Central Nevada: Spencer near Austin and Fish Lake Valley near Dyer, both handy stops on US 50 and US 95 road trips.
  • Southern Nevada: the canyon springs below Hoover Dam in Lake Mead National Recreation Area.

Not every spring on a map is open. Some are on private land, some are fenced to protect endangered fish, and some are simply too hot. Treat any spring you find on an app or old map as unconfirmed until you have checked who owns it and whether soaking is allowed.

Are Nevada hot springs safe to soak in?

The resort pools are maintained and tested; the wild springs are not, so soak in them knowing the risks. The National Park Service warns that natural springs can contain amoebas such as Naegleria, which in rare cases cause serious illness if water gets into the body through the nose. The usual advice is to keep your head above water, don't dunk, dive or splash, and never drink spring water.

Bacteria are another concern where cattle and wildlife share the water. In 2020 the BLM warned that Trego Hot Springs, about 20 miles northeast of Gerlach, had tested positive for E. coli, fecal coliforms and Vibrio cholerae, and advised the public to avoid bathing in it or touching the water (BLM). Skip any spring with posted warnings, and keep open cuts out of the water.

More practical rules for the backcountry:

  • Test the temperature first. Sources at springs like Spencer reach about 130 degrees. Feel the water with your hand and stay out of the source pool.
  • Limit your soak. Hot water and high-desert altitude dehydrate you fast. Drink plenty of water and don't mix soaking with alcohol.
  • Carry fuel, water and a spare tire. Services are far apart in central and northern Nevada, and cell coverage drops out for long stretches.
  • Watch the weather. Black Rock roads and the playa become impassable when wet, and desert canyons flash flood in storms.

What are the rules and etiquette at Nevada hot springs?

Treat a hot spring like a shared campsite: leave it cleaner than you found it. Travel Nevada's etiquette points are a good baseline:

  • Pass on glass. Use cans or unbreakable cups; broken glass in a pool is dangerous and hard to clean up.
  • Camp away from the water. Stay at least 100 yards from springs on BLM land like Spencer, and don't block the view or the path to the pools.
  • Pack it out. Take all trash home, close any gates you find closed and use a waste bag where there is no toilet.
  • Keep soap and sunscreen out. Several springs hold rare fish and snails.
  • Be courteous about clothing. There's no set statewide rule at wild springs, but resorts require swimsuits, and other soakers may be families.
  • Don't dam the flow. At Gold Strike the park says sandbags blocking the water can raise health risks.

When is the best time to visit hot springs in Nevada?

For most Nevada hot springs, fall through spring is the best time to go. The resorts near Reno and Carson City are open year-round, and a soak on a cold, clear winter night is the point. In southern Nevada, Gold Strike is open only from Oct. 1 to May 14, and fall and spring are the most comfortable months for the hike.

Remote springs depend on the roads. Spencer and Fish Lake Valley are reachable most of the year, though winter brings snow, ice and mud to dirt roads in central Nevada. For Soldier Meadows and the Black Rock country, late spring and fall usually offer the best balance of dry roads and mild temperatures. Nevada hot springs: FAQ

What is the closest hot spring to Las Vegas?

Gold Strike Hot Springs in Goldstrike Canyon near Boulder City, about an hour from the Strip. It is a very strenuous 5.3-mile round-trip canyoneering route with ropes and dry falls, and it is closed every year from May 15 to Sept. 30.

Are there hot springs near Reno?

Yes. Steamboat Hot Springs Healing Center is on the south edge of Reno, Carson Hot Springs is about 30 miles south in Carson City, and David Walley's Hot Springs Resort is near Genoa in the Carson Valley. All three are developed and open year-round.

How much does Carson Hot Springs cost?

A two-hour visit to the outdoor pools costs $18 for adults ages 12 to 54 and $16 for seniors and children 2 to 11. Private indoor pools cost $32 for adults and include the outdoor pools and sauna. Reservations are not taken.

Is Spencer Hot Springs free?

Yes. Spencer Hot Springs is on BLM land southeast of Austin, free and open 24 hours. There are no toilets, drinking water or trash service, so bring supplies and pack everything out.

Can you camp at Nevada hot springs?

At many public-land springs, yes. Spencer, Fish Lake Valley and Soldier Meadows all have free, first-come, first-served primitive camping. Keep your camp well away from the water, and note that overnight camping is not allowed in Goldstrike Canyon.

Are Nevada hot springs clothing optional?

There is no single statewide rule at wild springs, so be respectful of others who are there. Resorts such as Carson Hot Springs and Steamboat require swimsuits.
